## Article 1: coffee-mental-health.html
**Category:** Science
**Location:** United Kingdom
**Read time:** 3 min
**Headline:** Two or three cups of coffee a day was linked to better mental health in a 460,000-person study.
**Dek:** Researchers tracked participants for over thirteen years. The middle held up. The light drinkers and heavy drinkers missed out.

**Body:**

For thirteen and a half years, researchers at the UK Biobank watched what 461,586 people drank in the morning, and then watched what happened to them.

Specifically, they watched for mood disorders — depression, bipolar — and for stress disorders, the PTSD-and-acute-stress kind. None of the participants had been diagnosed with anything when the study began. They were just people aged 40 to 69, drinking their coffee, going about their lives.

The pattern, when it emerged, was J-shaped. Two or three cups a day was the floor of the curve. Drink less than that, and you didn't see the benefit. Drink more, and the protection started to fade. The middle held up.

The protective association showed up regardless of whether the coffee was instant, ground, or decaffeinated, which is the kind of finding that complicates the obvious explanation. If decaf is helping too, it's not just the caffeine doing the work. Researchers don't know what is.

The association was stronger in men, for reasons the paper doesn't try to settle.

Studies like this don't prove that coffee causes anything. They show what showed up next to what, across a very large group of people, over a very long period of time. People who drink moderate amounts of coffee tend to also do other things — sleep on a schedule, leave the house, sit with other humans in a kitchen — that can't be cleanly separated from the coffee itself.

Still. Of all the things that have been linked to better outcomes for the brain over the years, the morning cup keeps coming up. The middle of the curve, the people doing the boring, regular thing, doing it twice.

**Source:** Euronews, "Two to three cups of coffee a day linked to lower risk of mental health disorders, study finds," April 15, 2026; UK Biobank cohort study.

## Article 2: ai-bowel-cancer.html
**Category:** Health
**Location:** London & Dublin
**Read time:** 5 min
**Headline:** This drug only helps some bowel cancer patients. An AI tool can now tell doctors who.
**Dek:** Bevacizumab helps some patients and wrecks others with side effects. Researchers in London and Dublin think they've found a way to tell which group a person is in, before treatment starts.

**Body:**

When advanced bowel cancer spreads, the menu of treatment options gets short. One of the few drugs available on the NHS is bevacizumab, a targeted therapy that interferes with the blood supply tumors use to grow. It was approved for advanced bowel cancer in December 2025, after years of being out of reach for most NHS patients.

Here is the problem. Bevacizumab works for some people, and not for others. The ones it doesn't work for still get its side effects: high blood pressure, gastrointestinal trouble, blood clots. Until now, doctors couldn't tell in advance who was in which group. They prescribed it, watched, and waited.

Researchers at the Institute of Cancer Research in London and RCSI University in Dublin have built something that might change that. They call it PhenMap. It's an AI tool that pulls together the genetic profile of a tumor — what mutations are present, how the cells behave — and folds in clinical data like the patient's age, sex, and which side of the colon the cancer started on. From those inputs it produces a score, on a scale of one to a hundred, of how likely a patient is to benefit from the drug.

In a study of 117 European patients published this month in Scientific Reports, the highest-risk ten percent — the patients PhenMap flagged as least likely to respond — didn't respond. Not one of them. Among that group, every patient with a particular mutation in the BRAF gene had poor outcomes.

About 10,000 people in England are diagnosed with advanced bowel cancer each year. Of those, around 7,000 are eligible for bevacizumab, according to NICE. If a tool like PhenMap can reliably identify the patients who won't benefit, it could spare a meaningful number of people from a treatment that was never going to work for them.

**Pull quote:** "Our research uses advanced AI methods to pull together large amounts of complex data, helping us to spot patterns that would otherwise be impossible for a human to see, and to uncover the clues hidden within a patient's tumour." — Prof. Anguraj Sadanandam, Institute of Cancer Research

A study of 117 patients is small. Sadanandam is the first to say so. The tool needs to be validated in a larger cohort before clinicians could use it to guide actual prescribing, and the path through NICE-style health-technology appraisal is a long one. PhenMap won't be at the bedside next year, or probably the year after that.

But the principle is the interesting part. For most of the history of cancer treatment, drugs have been offered to broad groups of patients defined by where the tumor is and what stage it's reached. The question of which specific patients within that group will actually benefit has been answered, mostly, by trying it and seeing.

A tool that can answer that question before the trying begins is the kind of small, structural change that compounds.

**Source:** Institute of Cancer Research news release, April 13, 2026; Scientific Reports paper, "PhenMap and treatment response prediction in metastatic colorectal cancer."

## Article 3: school-lunch-standards.html
**Category:** Kindness
**Location:** England
**Read time:** 3 min
**Headline:** England is scrapping daily sugary snacks from school lunches for the first time in over a decade.
**Dek:** Deep-fried food banned outright. Sausage rolls demoted from daily to occasional. Three-quarters of parents told the government it was overdue.

**Body:**

The new school food standards, announced by the Department for Education on April 13, are the first major overhaul in more than ten years. Deep-fried food is gone — banned outright, no exceptions. The "grab and go" options that have anchored school menus for years, sausage rolls and pizza and the rest, can no longer appear daily. Sugary desserts are limited to once a week, and even then, the dessert has to be at least half fruit.

In their place: spaghetti bolognese, cottage pie with root-veg mash, jerk chicken with rice and peas, a roasted chickpea wrap. The sample menu the government released reads like the food page of a parenting magazine, which, given that one in three English children leaves primary school overweight or obese, is roughly the point.

A government poll found that three out of four parents thought the change was overdue. Fifty percent said they didn't get enough information about what their kids were being served at all.

The standards take effect in September 2027. Before then, there will be a nine-week consultation period, a national enforcement system, and the requirement that every school appoint a "lead governor" responsible for food and publish its menus online. Whether all of that survives contact with kitchen staff working from tight budgets in old facilities is another question.

**Pull quote:** "Today we are launching the most ambitious overhaul of school food in a generation, and it is long overdue." — Bridget Phillipson, Education Secretary

Tooth decay from high-sugar diets is currently the leading cause of hospital admissions for children aged five to nine in England. That is the kind of statistic that makes a sausage roll start to look different.

**Source:** Government press release, April 13, 2026; Department for Education School Food Standards consultation.

## Article 4: free-electricity-summer.html
**Category:** Technology
**Location:** United Kingdom
**Read time:** 3 min
**Headline:** Britain is going to give away free electricity this summer, as long as you use it at the right time.
**Dek:** Longer days, more rooftop solar, more wind capacity. The grid is on track to produce more electricity than the country can use.

**Body:**

The strange problem facing Britain's electricity grid this summer is that it might have too much.

Renewables produced 44% of UK electricity in 2025, up from 3% in 2000. Rooftop solar has more than doubled in the last decade, now around 22 gigawatts of installed capacity, equivalent to roughly thirty large coal plants. On a sunny, windy weekend in July, when factories are quiet and most people are outside, the grid can find itself with more electricity coming in than there is demand to absorb.

The current solution is to pay wind and solar farms to switch off. Britain spent £363 million on those payments in 2025 alone, plus another £1 billion replacing the curtailed clean energy with gas. The bill ends up in everyone's monthly statement.

So the National Energy System Operator, which is responsible for keeping the grid stable, is trying something different. Instead of paying generators to produce less, they're going to pay suppliers to encourage their customers to use more. Run the dishwasher. Charge the EV. Do the laundry on a Sunday afternoon. Suppliers can pass the incentive on as discounted rates, points, or in some cases, free electricity for a few hours.

It requires a smart meter and a participating supplier, of which there are a growing number. Octopus has been doing demand-shifting for years already. The new piece is that NESO will now actively signal these windows to suppliers across the country, rather than leaving them to figure it out alone.

The plan is also a small admission of where the grid is heading. For decades, the engineering question was: how do we make sure there's enough? The question, increasingly, is the opposite.

**Source:** National Energy System Operator Summer Outlook, April 14, 2026; Euronews; The Times.

## Article 5: us-road-deaths-2025.html
**Category:** Community
**Location:** United States
**Read time:** 3 min
**Headline:** U.S. road deaths in 2025 hit their second-lowest rate on record.
**Dek:** Still 36,640 people, which is not a small number. But it was a 6.7% drop from the previous year, and the fourth straight year of decline.

**Body:**

The number is 36,640 — that's how many people died in motor vehicle crashes on American roads in 2025, according to early estimates released by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration in April.

It is not a small number. It is, however, 6.7% smaller than the 39,254 people who died in 2024. And the fatality rate per hundred million vehicle miles traveled — the way road safety officials prefer to compare year over year, because Americans drove an extra 30 billion miles in 2025 — was 1.10. The lowest since 2014, the second-lowest in the more than fifty years the agency has been tracking it.

It is also the fourth consecutive year of decline. Road deaths spiked sharply during the early COVID pandemic, when emptier highways somehow produced more dangerous driving rather than less. The numbers have been falling since the second quarter of 2022, quarter after quarter, for thirteen straight quarters now.

NHTSA credits closer collaboration with state and local law enforcement, particularly around distracted driving, drunk driving, and seatbelt use. Fatalities decreased in 39 states plus DC and Puerto Rico. The remaining states — including New Mexico, which has the highest fatality rate in the country at 1.59 per 100 million miles — saw increases. Rhode Island had the lowest, at 0.47.

Of the people who died in 2024 vehicle crashes, nearly half of vehicle occupants weren't wearing a seatbelt, where seatbelt use was known.

The number is still 36,640.

**Source:** NHTSA press release and Crash Stats Early Estimates, April 1, 2026.

## Article 6: womens-health-strategy.html
**Category:** Good laws
**Location:** England
**Read time:** 4 min
**Headline:** England is restarting its women's health plan, with a focus on pain.
**Dek:** New national standards for pain relief during invasive gynecological procedures. A single referral point for endometriosis. A trial that could tie hospital funding to whether patients feel heard.

**Body:**

The first version of England's Women's Health Strategy was published in 2022. It listed the right problems — the average ten-year wait for an endometriosis diagnosis, the dismissal of women's pain during procedures like coil fittings and hysteroscopies, the gynecology waiting lists that had grown longer every year — but the changes that followed were modest. On April 14, the Department of Health and Social Care relaunched the strategy in a refreshed form, with sharper teeth.

The headline change is a new national standard of care for pain relief during invasive gynecological procedures. Patients undergoing IUD insertions, hysteroscopies, and similar treatments will be routinely offered effective pain management, addressing what the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists has spent years calling a culture of unmedicated pain.

Patients will also be directed through a single referral point for conditions like endometriosis, fibroids, and severe menstrual pain, instead of being passed from GP to specialist to diagnostic, sometimes for years. Gynecology waiting lists have already fallen by more than 30,000 since June 2024. There are still over 565,000 women on them.

The most experimental piece is a trial that would tie a portion of hospital funding to patient feedback — specifically, to whether women report feeling heard by the people treating them.

**Pull quote:** "Women's voices must be central to delivering effective, respectful and empathetic care. We need to hit medical misogyny where it hurts — the wallet." — Wes Streeting, Health and Social Care Secretary

Endometriosis UK welcomed the announcement and immediately added the obvious caveat: commitments without a delivery roadmap tend not to survive contact with reality. The strategy will need sustained funding, a clear timeline, and someone willing to publish the numbers when they don't move in the right direction.

The 2022 version did not have any of those things.

**Source:** GOV.UK press release and renewed Women's Health Strategy for England, April 14–15, 2026.

## Article 7: record-store-day-plant.html
**Category:** Culture (use a neutral color — Community orange or a secondary)
**Location:** Cardiff, Wales
**Read time:** 3 min
**Headline:** Record Store Day turned 19 on Saturday, and Robert Plant got named a Legend.
**Dek:** Thousands of independent record shops opened early. Plant got a permanent plaque at the world's oldest record store, in Cardiff.

**Body:**

Record Store Day is nineteen years old now. On Saturday morning, April 18, thousands of independent record stores around the world opened their doors early, mostly for the same group of people who have been doing this every year since the first one in 2008: collectors, completists, and the kind of music fan who still believes the cover matters.

The advice from one veteran behind the counter, repeated in basically every interview with a record-shop owner this week: bring a list. By lunchtime, the rare pressings are gone.

This year's slate ran to over 350 exclusive titles, organized by genre. Taylor Swift had a 7-inch. Bruno Mars, who served as the year's official ambassador, released a compilation of his collaborations. The Grateful Dead put out a five-LP set from a 1976 show. Slipknot finally released Look Outside Your Window, an album they recorded in 2008.

The bigger honor was the Record Store Legend award, which goes out rarely. Robert Plant got it. He is the third recipient, after Elton John in 2017 and Johnny Marr in 2025. A plaque was unveiled at Spillers Records in Cardiff, the world's oldest record shop, where Plant was photographed holding a copy of his own EP and looking, by all accounts, genuinely pleased.

**Pull quote:** "Record stores have always been a part of my life. For me, once you get to the physical record it's because you really want to know and be a part of what the artist was considering." — Robert Plant

U.S. vinyl revenue passed $1.04 billion in 2025 for the first time since 1983. The format that was supposed to die kept not dying.

**Source:** Billboard, April 16, 2026; Record Store Day UK press release; RIAA mid-year vinyl report.
